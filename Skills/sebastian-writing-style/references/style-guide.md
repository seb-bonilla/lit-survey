## 1. Governing principle

Write direct, logically staged scientific prose that prioritises accuracy, explicit relationships and information density. Lead the reader from the relevant problem or proposition to the mechanism, evidence, qualification and consequence. Do not require the reader to infer why a fact matters, how two statements are related or what conclusion follows.

The style is concise but not skeletal. Remove language that performs no scientific or rhetorical function, but retain every definition, condition, comparison, quantity and caveat needed for correct interpretation.

Use the ABC test:

- **Accurate:** the claim has the correct scope, conditions, comparison and certainty.
- **Brief:** no word, sentence or paragraph is present without a purpose.
- **Clear:** the reader can identify the point and follow the reasoning on first reading.

When these aims conflict, accuracy takes precedence, then clarity, then brevity.

## 2. Reasoning and structure

### Put the point first

Begin a section or paragraph with the proposition that gives the following material a purpose. This may be:

- the scientific problem;
- the physical relationship under investigation;
- the limitation of an existing method;
- the principal observation;
- the interpretation supported by the evidence; or
- the action and target of proposed work.

Avoid background-first paragraphs that accumulate facts before revealing why they matter. Supply context in the amount and at the point required to understand the argument.

### Build a causal chain

For introductions and strategic scientific prose, normally move through:

`broad need -> technical requirement -> present limitation -> specific gap -> approach -> expected or demonstrated consequence`

Every transition should answer why the next topic follows from the previous one. Use connective words only when their logical meaning is true:

- **however, whereas, in contrast:** genuine contrast;
- **therefore, thus, hence:** a conclusion follows;
- **because, owing to:** a supported cause;
- **although, despite:** a relevant concession;
- **for example:** an illustration, not additional proof;
- **in addition:** a separate but genuinely additive point.

Do not use formulaic transitions merely to make the prose appear connected.

### Respect prerequisite order

Introduce concepts in the order needed by a scientifically educated but non-specialist reader:

`basic object -> relevant components -> necessary distinction -> mechanism -> specialist detail`

Define a technical term at first meaningful use, in the shortest form that preserves comprehension. Delay secondary classifications until they affect the argument. Do not confuse brevity with leaving a dependency unexplained.

### End with a result

Where useful, finish a paragraph or section with the conclusion, decision, limitation or forward link the reader should retain. Do not merely repeat the opening sentence; advance it using the evidence just presented.

## 3. Paragraphs and sentences

### Paragraphs are units of reasoning

Give each substantive paragraph one central job. A strong paragraph normally contains:

1. **Point:** a direct opening proposition.
2. **Evidence or explanation:** the data, mechanism or reasoning needed to support it.
3. **Evaluation:** interpretation, comparison or qualification where relevant.
4. **Link:** the consequence or transition.

This is a guide rather than a rigid template. Methods paragraphs and short connective paragraphs may take a different form, but they must still have a clear purpose.

Split a paragraph when distinct claims compete for attention. Merge adjacent short paragraphs when they develop the same point and the break performs no useful function. In results sections, one experiment, simulation or coherent comparison will often form one paragraph.

### Sentence design

Prefer a concrete subject, a strong verb and an explicit complement. State the main clause before secondary conditions when doing so improves comprehension.

Use short or medium-length sentences by default. A longer sentence is acceptable when:

- it expresses one coherent relationship;
- the logical hierarchy of its clauses is clear;
- conditions sit next to the claims they qualify; and
- the reader does not need to retain several unfinished clauses.

Split a sentence when it contains independent claims, repeated conjunctions, nested qualifications or a main point that becomes difficult to locate.

### Active and passive voice

Use active voice when naming the responsible actor clarifies the scientific action:

- `We measured the carrier lifetime after annealing.`
- `Figure 3 compares the two interface treatments.`
- `The model predicts a reduction in resistive loss.`

Use passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant or deliberately backgrounded, especially for established procedures or work performed by others. Do not mechanically convert every passive construction.

### Information flow

Place familiar information before new information where possible. Keep a noun and its defining clause close together. Give pronouns, especially `this`, an identifiable referent. Repeat a technical noun when a pronoun would create ambiguity, but avoid repeated distinctive words where a clean alternative exists.

## 4. Scientific accuracy and evidence

### Bound every claim

Include the scientific boundaries that affect meaning:

- material, sample, population, interface or device;
- treatment or operating condition;
- measured quantity and unit;
- comparison case or baseline;
- relevant range or uncertainty; and
- scope and confidence of the conclusion.

Prefer `Lifetime increased from 60 to 660 microseconds after one ion drive-in cycle` to `Lifetime increased substantially`. Use a qualitative descriptor only when a quantity is unavailable or unnecessary, and provide its criterion or comparison when it matters.

### Separate observation and interpretation

Use a visible evidential progression:

`measurement -> observed trend -> comparison -> interpretation -> limitation`

Do not present association, sequence or model agreement as proof of causation. Calibrate language deliberately:

- **Direct evidence:** `shows`, `demonstrates`, `establishes`, `confirms`.
- **Supported inference:** `indicates`, `supports`, `is consistent with`.
- **Plausible explanation:** `suggests`, `could result from`, `may be attributed to`.
- **Working proposal:** `we propose`, `we hypothesise`.
- **Negative assessment:** `is unlikely because`.

Use the strongest verb justified by the evidence, not the strongest verb available.

### Comparisons

State what is being compared and on which basis. Avoid `higher`, `better`, `improved`, `reduced` or `more efficient` without an identifiable reference case. Distinguish absolute and relative changes. When reporting efficiency, make clear whether a percentage change is relative or in percentage points where ambiguity is possible.

### Literature and citations

Attach citations to the exact claims they support. Organise reviews around scientific questions or propositions, not a sequence of author summaries. Compare methods, assumptions, samples and conditions when these explain different results. Conclude what is established, uncertain, disputed or missing.

Do not invent a citation or allow polished prose to conceal a missing source. Do not describe a gap as valuable merely because few studies exist; explain why the missing knowledge matters and how the proposed work can address it.

## 5. Diction, tone and grammar

### Plain technical language

Preserve necessary disciplinary terminology, but use the simplest accurate surrounding language. Prefer verbs over abstract nominalisations:

- prefer `we measured` to `a measurement was undertaken`;
- prefer `temperature increased recombination` to `an increase in recombination was observed as a consequence of temperature variation`;
- prefer `the layer blocks ion migration` to `the layer provides ion-migration-blocking functionality`.

Avoid replacing a precise technical term merely to create lexical variety.

### Restrained modifiers

Delete adjectives and adverbs that advertise rather than inform. Words such as `remarkable`, `dramatic`, `ground-breaking`, `highly`, `extremely`, `vastly` and `significantly` require evidence, a defined statistical meaning or a genuine genre-specific reason.

In proposals, limited ambitious language is acceptable when immediately attached to a concrete innovation, measurable target, state-of-the-art comparison or beneficiary. The evidence should carry the claim.

### Tone

Use an authoritative, technically serious and impersonal tone. Confidence should come from explicit reasoning and evidence, not flourish. Be decisive about what the evidence permits and equally clear about uncertainty.

Avoid conversational fillers, rhetorical questions, slogans and inflated claims. Use first person naturally when it clarifies agency. Do not hide a research decision behind unnecessary impersonality.

### Conventions

Unless another house style is specified:

- use British spelling: `optimise`, `characterise`, `minimise`, `behaviour`;
- use sentence case for headings;
- define acronyms at first meaningful use and use them consistently;
- do not capitalise an expanded common noun merely because its acronym is capitalised;
- keep terminology, symbols, abbreviations and hyphenation consistent;
- use a space between a number and its unit: `100 MW`, `25 °C`;
- preserve conventional compound units and mathematical notation; and
- use punctuation to clarify syntax, not to rescue an overloaded sentence.

## 6. Figures, tables, equations and quantities

### Figures

Introduce a figure by stating its scientific content, not only its existence. A useful sequence is:

1. explain what is displayed and why it is relevant;
2. guide the reader through panels, axes, regions or components in a deliberate order;
3. identify the trend or comparison that supplies evidence;
4. interpret its meaning; and
5. state the conclusion supported by the figure.

Prefer `Figure 2 shows that lifetime reaches a maximum after the first charging cycle` to `The results are shown in Figure 2`.

Captions should identify the samples, variables, conditions, symbols and analysis needed to understand the figure without reconstructing the methods section.

### Tables

Introduce the purpose of a table before it appears. Use descriptive headings with units. Extract important comparisons in the prose rather than repeating every cell. Keep labels and presentation consistent.

### Equations

Before a displayed equation, state what relationship it expresses and cite its source if borrowed. After it, define every symbol at first use and explain why the equation is needed. Later references should state the relevance of the numbered equation rather than merely pointing to it.

### Quantities

Use numbers to establish scale, performance, feasibility or contrast. Preserve significant figures appropriate to the evidence. Check units, signs, subscripts, superscripts and dimensional consistency. Do not add false precision.

## 7. Genre-specific guidance

### Abstract

Create a self-contained sequence:

`accessible context -> precise problem -> approach -> principal result -> scientific advance or consequence`

State the main result directly and quantitatively where possible. Do not spend most of the abstract on background. Avoid undefined acronyms and claims that exceed the paper.

### Introduction

Use a funnel, not a catalogue:

1. establish the broader scientific or societal need;
2. explain why the research area is relevant to that need;
3. identify the specific limitation or unresolved question;
4. define the gap and why it matters; and
5. state the contribution, objective and strategy.

Each narrowing step must be justified. Do not jump from a broad problem directly to a specialist device or material without explaining the connection.

### Methods

Prioritise reproducibility. State materials, parameters, procedures, models, analysis and conditions with the precision required by a specialist. Distinguish what was performed in the present work from adopted methods. Use simple past for completed procedures unless journal convention requires otherwise.

### Results

Organise results in the strongest logical order rather than automatically following chronology. Begin each unit with the question, comparison or result being addressed. Describe observations before advancing explanations when conflation would overstate the evidence. Integrate figures as evidence. Report relevant negative or failed results when they affect interpretation.

### Discussion

Explain what the results mean, why they matter, how they compare with expectations or literature, and which limitations or alternatives remain. Do not repeat the Results section in different words. Move from the specific evidence to the justified broader implication without exceeding it.

### Conclusion

State what the work showed, what follows from it and what can now be done or tested. Synthesis is preferable to repetition. Do not introduce unsupported new claims or generic statements about future work.

### Review article or literature review

Organise around questions, mechanisms, material families, trade-offs or other scientific propositions. Use papers as evidence within that structure. Compare like with like and explain disagreements. Distinguish intrinsic constraints from limitations that improved fabrication or measurement could overcome. End major sections with a judgement about what is known and what remains unresolved.

### Research proposal

Make the chain from need to deliverable explicit:

`importance -> obstacle -> knowledge gap -> proposed advance -> method -> measurable target -> benefit`

Define objectives as assessable outcomes, normally beginning with active verbs such as `determine`, `establish`, `quantify`, `demonstrate` or `validate`. Separate ambition from unsupported promotion. Show feasibility through preliminary evidence, relevant capability, specific methods and credible fallback routes. Explain the consequence of success at scientific, technological or societal level without repeating generic impact claims.

### Strategic case or programme document

Quantify the scale of the need. Show how programme components exchange evidence, parameters or outputs rather than merely stating that they are interconnected. Link materials, devices, systems and consequences explicitly. Define milestones so that progress can be assessed.

### Thesis or scientific report

Write for a scientifically educated reader who may not know the specialist field. Maintain prerequisite order across chapters and sections. Use cross-references when earlier definitions or evidence are necessary. Each section should have an identifiable purpose and a closing conclusion or transition where useful.

## 8. Editing tests

Apply these tests before delivering prose.

### Plain proposition test

State privately, in one direct sentence, what each paragraph needs the reader to understand. If this cannot be done, the paragraph's purpose is unclear. If the direct proposition exists but is hidden, move it earlier and simplify the surrounding structure.

### Scientific boundary test

For each major claim, ask:

- To which system does it apply?
- Under which conditions?
- Compared with what?
- How was it determined?
- With what confidence?

Restore any missing answer that changes interpretation.

### Logic test

Check every connector. Would the relationship remain true if the connector were replaced by its literal meaning? If not, restructure the sentences rather than selecting a different decorative transition.

### Deletion test

Delete a word or sentence provisionally. If scientific meaning, logical continuity, necessary emphasis and reader orientation remain unchanged, leave it deleted.

### Evidence test

Mark each statement as observation, established knowledge, cited prior result, inference, hypothesis or planned action. Ensure its wording and tense match that status.

### Reader test

Check that a related-discipline scientist can identify the subject, action and consequence on first reading and has received the definitions needed before specialist use.

## 9. Characteristic patterns

Use these as adaptable reasoning patterns, not fixed phrases.

- **Problem and consequence:** `X remains limited by Y, preventing Z.`
- **Contrast and decision:** `Although X provides A, it introduces B. We therefore use Y.`
- **Evidence and interpretation:** `X increased from A to B, indicating Y.`
- **Qualification:** `This explanation is unlikely because...`
- **Requirement and target:** `To achieve X, Y must remain below Z.`
- **Figure-led reasoning:** `Figure X compares A and B. The reduction in C indicates D.`
- **Proposal action:** `We will determine how X controls Y by measuring Z.`
- **Gap and contribution:** `Existing methods achieve X but cannot provide Y under Z conditions. Here, we...`

Vary wording naturally. The purpose of these patterns is to expose the logic, not to create formulaic prose.

## 10. Prohibited habits

Revise or remove:

- generic opening statements that delay the scientific problem;
- decorative adjectives and intensifiers without evidence;
- vague nouns and pronouns such as `various factors`, `aspects` or an unclear `this`;
- abstract nominalisations that hide the actor or physical relationship;
- unsupported causal claims;
- claims of novelty, importance or completeness without a comparison or source;
- paper-by-paper literature catalogues without synthesis;
- repeated conclusions or paraphrases that add no meaning;
- sentence fragments presented as technical concision;
- long sentences containing several independent claims;
- transitions that misstate the logical relationship;
- premature acronyms, classifications or specialist detail;
- figures mentioned without interpretation;
- conclusions stronger than the evidence;
- promotional or formulaic generated prose; and
- shortening that deletes necessary conditions, quantities, scope or caveats.
