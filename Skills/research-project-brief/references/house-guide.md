# Lean Research Project Brief House Guide

The brief is a compact scientific direction document, not a schedule or management plan. Its job is to tell the reader:

1. What is the technical problem?
2. What must the project establish?
3. What work needs to be conducted?

Include nothing that does not help answer those questions or satisfy a supplied form.

## Voice

- Direct, technically precise and economical.
- British English.
- Concrete verbs: measure, determine, compare, optimise, model, correlate, reproduce, validate.
- Specific materials, mechanisms, variables, methods and observables.
- Ambitious only where the proposed work supports the claim.

Avoid scene-setting, promotional language, generic sustainability claims and explanations that an appropriate research audience does not need.

## Default structure

Use only the sections that add information.

### Title

Name the material or device, the research action and the purpose. Keep it to one line where possible.

### Project overview

Use one to three short paragraphs:

- Define the technical context and problem.
- State the main approach.
- Mention secondary or computational strands only if central to the project.

Do not preview every later bullet or repeat the outputs.

### Aim

Use one sentence. A useful form is:

> Establish how [controllable variables] affect [key property or mechanism], to enable [research or device objective].

### Core research questions or targets

Use a short bullet list. Include only questions the proposed work can answer. Combine overlapping questions.

### Research strands

For each strand:

- give it a short descriptive heading;
- add one sentence of purpose only if the heading is insufficient;
- list the essential work as concise action bullets; and
- state an output only when it clarifies the endpoint.

Keep secondary work visibly conditional. Do not give it equal weight to the main strand.

### Proposed experiments or modelling work

List the minimum experiments or tasks needed to start and progress the project. Organise by scientific activity, not by week or calendar period, unless timing is explicitly requested.

Include essential controls, comparisons and measurements. Omit routine administration, inductions, meetings, file handling, reporting and scheduling.

### Outputs and resources

Include only concrete outputs or genuinely useful starting sources. Do not restate every strand in different words.

## Compression rules

- One idea per bullet.
- Prefer a short bullet to a paragraph when explanation is unnecessary.
- Delete sentences that merely announce what the next section says.
- Delete background already implicit in the title or audience.
- Mention a method once, at the point where its purpose is clearest.
- Combine method lists when the same measurements apply across several samples.
- Replace long validation workflows with the essential comparison or benchmark.
- Avoid exhaustive lists of possible variables; name the priority variables and say “other relevant deposition conditions” if needed.
- Do not add detail merely because it may be useful later.

## What not to add by default

- Week-by-week or month-by-month plans
- Milestones, gates or schedules
- Project metadata not supplied by the user
- Meetings, inductions or facility-booking steps
- Sample naming and file-management procedures
- General data-management requirements
- Version-control or reporting instructions
- Training inventories
- Risk registers and contingency plans
- Long lists of assumptions, placeholders or open items
- Separate sections for outputs, impact and wider value when one concise endpoint is sufficient

Add any of these only when requested or when required by the destination template.

## Scientific quality

Brevity must not remove the research logic:

**Problem → target → work → interpretable result**

- Pair each method with the property or mechanism it will resolve.
- Distinguish established facts from hypotheses.
- Keep controls and comparators when they are necessary to interpret results.
- Do not invent missing details.
- Keep one governing aim; place optional avenues beneath it.

## Brief types

### Working brief

Focus on the overview, aim, key questions, research strands and essential experiments. Questions and conditional avenues are acceptable. Do not turn it into an operational plan.

### Submission brief

Follow the supplied form exactly. Compress the same scientific logic into the required summary and project-description fields. Add remit, training, impact, TRI or IP text only when the form asks for it.

## Brevity check

Before delivery, ask:

- Can any paragraph become one sentence or a few bullets?
- Is any point repeated in another section?
- Does every sentence define the problem, target, work or result?
- Have scheduling and administrative details been excluded?
- Are secondary strands shorter than the primary work?
- Could the reader identify what needs to be done after one quick read?

Delete anything that fails these checks.
